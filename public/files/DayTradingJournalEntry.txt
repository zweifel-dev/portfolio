My Day Trading Journey: From a Movie Scene to Real Trades
June 11, 2025
Why I Started Day Trading
The first time I saw anyone day trade was in Paranormal Activity. The movie scared me, but the trading stuck with me. Getting started is overwhelming. News headlines, brokers, apps, charts and candles all come at you at once, and at first you don't know what most of them are, let alone how they fit together.
As I write this, I've spent about two weeks learning to day trade. That isn't long, but I've been swing trading (buying a stock and holding it for the longer term) for more than 10 years. I also tend to go all in when I start something new, and this has had my full attention.
I'm approaching it the way I'd learn any hard skill: read a lot, practice, track the results and ask a lot of questions. For the questions, I lean on ChatGPT and Claude as much as on books.

Charts and Indicators
Before picking stocks, you need to know how to read a chart. That was the first thing I learned.
Reading Candlestick Charts
The most common chart is made of candles, each covering a fixed slice of time. A candle summarizes every order filled during its slice with four numbers. A chart from 9:30 to 10:30 a.m. at a 1-minute interval has 60 candles, one per minute, with time on the x-axis and price on the y-axis.
The four numbers are:
•	The opening price for that period (the first filled trade in it)
•	The closing price for that period (the last filled trade in it)
•	The highest price traded in that period
•	The lowest price traded in that period
The body of the candle spans the open and close. A thin line called a wick (or shadow) runs up to the high and down to the low.
A single candle can hold anywhere from thousands to billions of orders between its high and low. My first question was why the chart doesn't show the average price inside each candle. Most of the volume could have traded up in one of the wicks, and the candle wouldn't tell you. I don't know yet whether that matters for analysis. What charts do offer is a set of indicators.
Technical Indicators
VWAP (Volume Weighted Average Price): The day's average price, weighted by volume. It's an intraday measure that resets at 9:30 every morning. On the chart it shows as three lines: the VWAP itself and one standard deviation on either side. When the price breaks through a line, that can signal momentum in that direction. When the price nears a line and "bounces" back, the line is acting as support or resistance.
EMA (Exponential Moving Average): A moving average that gives recent prices more weight than older ones, so it reacts faster to price changes. The number after it (EMA 9, EMA 20) is how many candles back it averages.
A simple moving average (SMA) weights every candle equally; the EMA weights recent candles exponentially more. Search for the best EMA length and you'll find anything from 4 to 200. The common ones are 4, 5, 8, 9, 10, 12, 20, 26, 50, 100 and 200. Short ones catch moves and new trends quickly. Lengths from 20 to 50 are used to confirm a trend. 100 and 200 show the broad direction and matter more to swing traders than day traders.
MACD (Moving Average Convergence Divergence): A momentum indicator built from two EMAs. It gets its own chart with three parts. The MACD line is the 12-period EMA minus the 26-period EMA. The signal line is a 9-period EMA. The histogram shows the gap between the MACD line and the signal line, so you can see the averages converge or diverge.
The crossovers are what to watch. When the MACD line crosses above the signal line, that can mean a bullish trend (a buy signal); when it crosses below, a bearish one (a sell signal). A cross of the zero line, up or down, shows momentum in that direction.
RSI (Relative Strength Index): The last indicator I watch, and another momentum indicator. It measures how fast and how far the price has moved, on a scale of 0 to 100, to judge whether a stock is overbought or oversold. Above 70 means overbought, and a pullback may be coming. Below 30 means oversold, and a rebound may be coming. Between 30 and 70 is neutral, and 50 means no trend. Traders disagree on whether RSI leads or lags the price.
None of these indicators can tell you on its own where a stock will go. Most of them lag: they describe what already happened, where a leading indicator would move first.
Candlestick Patterns
Next are candlestick patterns: arrangements of a few candles that have tended to come before certain price moves. There are hundreds of them, including the doji, hammer, shooting star, morning star, evening star, hanging man, inverted hammer, three white soldiers, three black crows and spinning top. I've read about more than I can count and can't memorize them all, so I bought a cheat sheet of the common ones to hang on the wall next to my desk.
Patterns are controversial among traders. I've literally heard someone say, "Patterns like doji and dead cat bounce or whatever it is, is total BS. You need to use data points not astrology." The names don't help their case, but the patterns are data. Each candle is four data points (open, high, low and close), and a pattern is a particular arrangement of those points that has historically been followed by a particular move.
fivehundred.co studied the predictive power of candlestick patterns over 40 years of data on major stock indices and large-cap stocks. The patterns were close to 70% accurate 30 to 40 years ago and were still above 60% in 2024. A pattern counted as accurate if it predicted a move of at least one average true range (ATR) within the next three bars.
Paper Trading vs. Real Trading
Paper trading, also called trading in a sim, lets you practice on live market data without risking or making money. You place orders as if they were real, and the platform tracks how you would have done. It has drawbacks, but the benefits are worth far more.
I started paper trading in thinkorSwim before moving any money into the account. I watched a couple of their tutorial videos, and it all seemed easy enough. I paper traded for two days while I kept reading and picking up terms.
Where the Sim Falls Short
The sim's drawbacks show up once you trade real money. Fear, greed and FOMO don't exist with fake money, so you make different decisions: you hold positions longer, take bigger risks and stay calm when a trade goes against you.
The bigger problem, which I didn't notice until I traded real money, is slippage. In the sim, a market order fills at exactly the price on the screen, every time. In the real market you might see $10.50, hit buy and get filled at $10.52 or $10.53, especially on fast, volatile stocks.
That taught me bad habits. In the sim I'd set my stop loss exactly 2% below my entry, because I knew I'd get filled at that price. In real trading, slippage could make that stop hit 2.5% or 3% below my entry, especially when a stock gapped down. So I kept tightening my stops, thinking that was good risk management. It wasn't.
I'd get into a position, the stock would dip a little from normal price action plus slippage, and I'd get stopped out too early. Thirty seconds later I'd watch it reverse and hit my original profit target. Slippage was eating into the risk I'd planned for and pushing me out of trades early.
After two days of paper trading I thought I had a handle on it. Then I put real money in and found I'd learned nothing about the two things that matter most: managing my emotions, and real market conditions like slippage that the sim leaves out.
Order Types and Risk Management
With the charts covered, the last thing to learn before trading is the order types. There's a lot more to it than "buy" and "sell."
TYPE	BUY	SELL
LIMIT	Buy at this exact price	Sell at this exact price
MARKET	Buy at the current market price	Sell at the current market price
STOP-LOSS	Buy when the price reaches a specific price. (Usually used on short positions)	Sell when the price reaches a specific price. Can be used to take profit at certain targets, or to get out of a position to lower your loss.
TRAILING-STOP	A buy order that triggers when the price moves by a set amount ($, %, or ticks), with the trigger price following the market. For example, if stock X is at $20 and you think it will rise 5%, you place a 5% buy trailing stop at $21. If X rises to $21, the order triggers. If X first falls to $15, the trigger falls with it to $15.75 (5% above the market), and the buy triggers when X climbs back to $15.75. The trigger only moves down, never up.	A sell order that triggers when the price moves by a set amount ($, %, or ticks), with the trigger price following the market. For example, if stock X is at $100 and you set a $10 trailing stop, the order starts at $90. If X rises to $110, the trigger rises to $100. When X falls back to the trigger, the order executes. The trigger only moves up, never down.
Advanced Order Types
OCO (one cancels other) order: Two or more linked orders. When one fills, the others are canceled automatically. You can use it to set a profit target (sell once you reach a certain gain) and a stop loss (sell if the price drops, to limit the loss) at the same time.
Bracket order: A buy order that places an OCO order automatically once it fills, so your risk management is set up in advance. For example, you place a bracket order to buy X at $5 with a 10% take profit and a 5% stop loss. Once the buy fills, it creates an OCO with a sell at $5.50 (10% above $5) and a sell at $4.75 (5% below $5). Only one of them fills, and the other is canceled.
Risk and Reward
When you buy, you should know your risk and your reward before you enter. Say a stock is at $1.0000 a share and you're using a 1:2 risk/reward ratio (risk 1 to make 2) with a 5% stop loss. Your stop is at $0.9500 and your profit target is $1.1000: 5 cents of risk for 10 cents of reward. At 1:2, you only need to be right 33% of the time to break even. You obviously want to do better than that, and not every trade risks the same dollar amount.
Right now, I just want my trades to end up profitable (green). Looking back at my first trades, I hadn't thought this through, even though I'd read about it. Sometimes I'd buy expecting momentum to carry the price up, it would dip a little, and I'd sell instead of holding until my stop loss. If I'd waited another 30 seconds, it would have gone back up past my profit target. Other times it would drop past my stop, and I'd sit there too long before taking the loss, so I lost more than I should have.
Using ATR for Stops and Targets
That raises the question of where to set your stop loss and profit target. I'm still working out the best approach. There are lots of opinions, most based on "support" and "resistance" levels such as the premarket high and low, VWAP and its bands, and trend lines drawn across peaks. One measure I'm trying is the stock's ATR (average true range). ATR measures volatility by averaging the price range over a period, usually 14 days. Knowing how far a stock normally moves helps you set a stop that won't get hit too early. ATR lags, though: it's calculated from past prices, so it can't see a sudden spike or drop in volatility coming.
My plan is to draw a horizontal line on the chart at my entry price minus one ATR and another at my entry plus two ATRs. Those are my exits. If a candle opens or closes past either line (wicks don't count), I get out.
Finding Stocks to Trade (Screeners)
Next is choosing which stock to trade, which has been one of the harder parts so far. The books I've read for retail day traders agree on a few factors. (Retail traders are individuals like you and me; institutional traders are firms.)
Share Price
First is the share price. I group stocks into these ranges:
•	$1.00 or less
•	$1.00 to $5.00
•	$5.00 to $10.00
•	$10.00 to $20.00
•	$20.00 to $50.00
•	$50.00 to $100.00
•	$100 or more
The cheaper the stock, the more shares your money buys. That doesn't automatically mean bigger profits or losses. Many of the stocks I've seen under $5 trade in fractions of a penny (for example, $1.2473 a share), so they can move a lot within those fractions, while an $8.00 stock moves in whole cents. The smallest amount a stock's price can move is called a tick.
Back to the $1.00 stock. Making $0.10 a share doesn't sound worth the effort, and this is where "it takes money to make money" comes in. Say you buy at $1.00 with a 5-cent stop loss and a 1:2 ratio, the price hits $1.10, and you sell everything. With 100 shares, you paid $100 and sell for $110: you risked $5 to make $10. With 10,000 shares, you risk $500 to make $1,000, but you need $10,000 of buying power to get in.
What I Screen For
A screener filters the market for stocks that meet your criteria. The two I care about most are relative volume and the gap.
Relative volume: Is the stock trading more than usual? If it normally trades 500K shares a day and today it's at 10M, relative volume is 20x.
Gap: The difference between the previous day's close and the current price (after hours, premarket or during the day), up or down. You want high relative volume and a big gap. Together they tell you the stock is moving.
Day traders usually add three more filters: average volume, market cap and float.
Average volume: How much the stock normally trades. If it's too low, a 10x relative volume is still only 10 times a small number. I look for at least 500K shares a day.
Market cap: Roughly tied to share price. I stick to micro- and small-cap stocks, under $2B. That's generally where retail traders are active, while institutions with more capital trade the larger stocks.
Float: The number of a company's shares available for public trading. This one took me a while. At first I assumed a high float was better, since there would be more shares to trade. But a lower float, with fewer shares available, tends to make a stock more volatile, and volatility is what day traders want. Float is the total share count minus shares held by insiders, restricted shares (like employee shares) and shares held by institutions. Float rotation is how many times the float changes hands during the day; a high rotation means the stock is being actively traded, which makes it a better intraday candidate. Under 100M float is good, under 50M is better, and under 10M is ideal.
Catalysts
One more thing you usually can't filter for is a catalyst: the reason the stock is moving. Is the company acquiring another company, or being acquired? Did it release earnings? Get FDA approval? Hire a new executive? Finding the news is easy, since every platform I considered has a news scanner for a given stock.
My First Trades
As I mentioned, I funded my Webull account. I made two deposits: $26K by bank transfer and $2K by debit card. The debit card deposit is instant and becomes buying power the next day, with a $10K daily limit. The $26K transfer takes 5 to 10 days to become usable for trading, though it counts toward the account's equity right away.
I needed at least $25K in the account to day trade freely. If you make more than a few day trades in a short window, your broker flags you as a "pattern day trader," and pattern day traders must have $25K in the account at the start of each day. If you drop below that, the account is locked or limited in how many trades you can make. I deposited a little more than the minimum to leave room for swings.
While I waited for the $26K to clear, I traded with the $2K. Because the larger transfer was already scheduled, I could day trade without worrying about the pattern day trader limit.
My First Three Days
My first trades were on Friday, June 6. It didn't go well: I finished red at -$142.76. Monday I was green at +$39.38, and Tuesday green again at +$166.03.
I learned a few things fast. First, the market doesn't care what you want. "You trade the market you are in, not the one you want." Looking back, I can see what I did wrong on each trade. Either I panic-sold at the wrong moment, or I bought at the peak even though the patterns and indicators suggested a reversal (the price turning back down instead of continuing up).
Part of how I worked that out was a trade journal: recording every order and trade, including what the indicators showed and why I got in and out. Then I can compare the trades that made money with the ones that lost.
Building a Trade Journal
I tried a few ways to keep the journal. The first was Excel, which took a lot of manual work and digging up data after the fact. Webull can export all your past orders to CSV, and I could add the indicators, patterns and anything else relevant by hand, but it felt old-fashioned and slow. Next I tried Notion, and I was leaning that way until I made a profit-and-loss chart and Notion asked me to upgrade to a paid plan. There are also sites built for trade journaling that connect to your broker or import your trades. I started a TraderSync trial, but it also needs a monthly subscription. I'm trying to avoid overhead until I'm consistently profitable.
Doing It as a Software Engineer
So I switched approaches. Between my years as a software engineer and my data science classes, I wanted to automate as much as I could. Whenever I tell people about an app idea, someone says, "Just build an app that takes market data, runs machine learning on it, and makes you rich." As if nobody had thought of that.
Some institutional trading firms do something like it, though their systems are mostly rules built from experience rather than real machine learning. Some day traders even learn to recognize how those big firms trade and profit from the price swings their volume causes. The best-known example is the 2007 to 2008 housing crash. Algorithmic trading didn't cause the crisis (subprime mortgages and credit derivatives did), but it added to the market's volatility during the crash. By 2009, algorithms accounted for 60 to 73% of all equity trading volume, which makes them very hard for retail traders to compete with. They also leave predictable patterns, though, and a human trader who knows what to look for can take advantage of them.
There are whole communities devoted to algorithmic trading now. I bought the two books listed above on the subject. I don't expect to be the one who finally cracks it (though maybe I will), but AI and ML are moving fast, and I think it's only a matter of time.
My Python Journal
For now, I use Python to collect the data. I wrote an app that reads the CSV Webull exports into a data frame, then calls Yahoo Finance (a free Python library for historical stock data) to fill in the market conditions at the time of each order. For each trade it records the symbol, profit or loss, cap category, entry and exit times and prices, quantity, P/L percentage, how many minutes I held, float, average volume, relative volume, gap percent, price range percent, intraday volatility, RSI, EMA 9 and the price relative to it, VWAP and the price relative to it, MACD, the MACD signal, a bullish MACD flag, an entry and exit timing analysis, and a trade quality score (how many of my setup criteria the trade met). It saves the results to a CSV for analysis.
Reviewing past wins and mistakes should help me find the setups where I make money and the ones to avoid. The fun part is that I don't have to do that analysis myself. I give the CSV to Claude and to ChatGPT with this prompt: "As an expert financial day trader who specializes in small and micro-cap intraday trading, analyze and provide insights from this trading journal."
I only have three days of data so far, and the analysis will get better as I trade more. It has already found indicator readings where I shouldn't have entered or exited, and patterns like "in these scenarios, you are most profitable." I plan to keep doing this every day. For the record, I pay for ChatGPT Pro and Claude Max, but for my day job, where they help me draft and review emails, write job descriptions and summarize meeting transcripts.
Taxes
Two more things, both about taxes. You need to understand how day trading profits and losses affect your tax bill at the end of the year.
The Wash Sale Rule
The first is the wash sale rule. If you sell a stock at a loss and buy the same stock again within 30 days, you can't claim that loss as a deduction. On top of that, you can only deduct $3,000 a year of net trading losses against other income; anything beyond that rolls over to the next year.
For day traders, this can get ugly. Say you made two trades in stock XYZ: one gained $4,000 and the other lost $5,000. You'd expect to owe nothing, since you lost $1,000 overall. But if the wash sale rule applies, the loss can be disallowed, and you're taxed on the $4,000 gain anyway. You can end up paying tax on money you never actually made.
Trader Tax Status
The way around this is to qualify for Trader Tax Status and make the mark-to-market election under Section 475(f). With it, you treat every position as if you sold it on the last day of the year and are taxed on the net result. In the example above, you'd report a $1,000 loss instead of a $4,000 gain.
With Trader Tax Status, the IRS treats you as a business rather than an investor. That means:
•	Wash sale rules don't apply
•	The $3,000 annual loss limit doesn't apply
•	You can deduct trading expenses (platform fees, data costs, a home office)
•	All gains and losses are ordinary income (no capital gains treatment)
To qualify, you need to trade often (typically 720 or more trades a year), hold positions for short periods and earn substantial income from trading. The IRS doesn't publish exact requirements, so it's somewhat subjective.
Timing
The catch is that you have to make the election ahead of time, by the April 15 tax deadline for the previous year's return. It's June 2025 and I've already filed my 2024 taxes, so it's too late for 2025. I plan to file the election in December for the 2026 tax year.
Miss that deadline and you could pay tax on gains you never really had, which can mean thousands of dollars. If you're serious about day trading, plan your taxes ahead.
